# Squares forming a nonpowerful subgroup

**Result:** the main universal assertion and the 2-group case of L. Wilson's
[Kourovka Notebook problem 21.137](https://alglog.org/21tkt.pdf)
(21st edition, printed p. 181; UnsolvedMath record 2646) are false.
The separate odd-prime question is not settled here.
No prior resolution was found in a bounded search on 10 September 2026;
priority is unconfirmed.
The [expanded prior-art check](prior-art.md) identifies two relevant older
papers whose full texts remain unchecked. This is a material gap in any
claim of novelty.

## The group

Let $D=\langle r,s\mid r^4=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle$ be the dihedral group
of order eight, and put $z=r^2$. Its centre is $Z=\{1,z\}$.
Take
$$
G=(D\times D)\rtimes\langle t\rangle,\qquad
t^2=1,\quad t(x,y)t=(y,x).
$$
Thus $G=D\wr C_2$ has order $128$.
We will show that its squares are exactly
$$
S=\{(g,gz^\epsilon):g\in D,\ \epsilon\in\{0,1\}\}.
$$
This is a subgroup isomorphic to $D\times C_2$, via
$(g,z^\epsilon)\mapsto(g,gz^\epsilon)$, and is therefore nonabelian.

## Identify every square

For elements of the base group and its other coset, respectively,
$$
(x,y)^2=(x^2,y^2),\qquad ((x,y)t)^2=(xy,yx).
$$
Every square in $D$ lies in $Z$, so the first expression lies in $Z\times Z$,
which is contained in $S$. In the second expression, $xy$ and $yx$ are
conjugate. Conjugate elements of $D$ differ by an element of $Z$, so this
expression also lies in $S$.

Conversely, take $(g,gz^\epsilon)\in S$.

- If $g\notin Z$, its conjugacy class is exactly $\{g,gz\}$.
  Choose $x$ with $x^{-1}gx=gz^\epsilon$ and set $y=x^{-1}g$.
  Then $xy=g$, $yx=gz^\epsilon$, and $((x,y)t)^2=(g,gz^\epsilon)$.
- If $g\in Z$, both coordinates lie in $Z$. Each element of $Z$ is a
  square in $D$ ($1=1^2$ and $z=r^2$), so their ordered pair is a square
  in the base group $D\times D$.

Hence the set of squares in $G$ is precisely the subgroup $S$.

## Exponent and the obstruction

The subgroup $S\cong D\times C_2$ has exponent four. Since every square
in $G$ belongs to $S$, every element of $G$ has eighth power one.
The element $(r,1)t$ squares to $(r,r)$ of order four, so $G$ has exponent
exactly eight.

The squares
$$
((r,1)t)^2=(r,r),\qquad ((s,1)t)^2=(s,s)
$$
do not commute. Thus $S$ is not abelian.
A finite 2-group $P$ is powerful when $[P,P]\le P^4$, where $P^4$ is
generated by its fourth powers. Here $S^4=1$ but $[S,S]\ne1$,
so $S$ is **not powerful**.

This answers the main “must the power subgroup be powerful?” question
negatively, as well as its explicit exponent-eight special case.
It makes no claim about groups of odd prime-power order.
